The Mechanical Clock

Monasteries → Cities → Industrial Society
Step 1 (Benedictine Monasteries, 7th–12th C. CE)
The desire for spiritual order and collective discipline in Benedictine monasteries required strict temporal regulation
Pope Sabinian decreed in the 7th century that monastery bells ring seven times in twenty-four hours
Within monastery walls, time was stripped of natural irregularities and replaced with mechanical regularity
Step 2 (European Towns, 13th C. CE)
Invention of the mechanical escapement allows weight-driven clock towers to strike hours regardless of weather
The technical invention of the escapement, a mechanism that releases weight-driven gear energy in measured, incremental beats, allowed clocks to run reliably without water
Within a century of its invention, mechanical clocks moved from secluded monasteries into town squares and cathedral steeples
Town bells struck hours that everyone nearby could hear, transforming time from an internal religious habit into a shared public obligation
Step 3 (Civic Squares, 14th C. CE)
Clocks move to public city towers, replacing natural temporal rhythms with standardized work hours for town merchants and laborers
Rising merchant, guild, and trade networks adopted synchronized time to measure labor, organize markets, and calculate wages
Time became detached from natural human needs and organic cycles and "To become as regular as clockwork" became the urban ideal
Step 4 (Global Modernity, 16th C. CE onward)
Clockmakers miniaturized mechanisms into pocket watches and domestic clocks, diffusing time-keeping from public towers into individual homes and pockets
Clock time became so thoroughly embedded in daily existence that humans viewed it as an unavoidable fact of nature rather than a human invention
